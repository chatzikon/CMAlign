Analyze this image as an investigation-support assistant.

Report only visually grounded findings that could matter to an investigator,
security analyst, or threat assessor.

For each relevant finding, use exactly this format:

Observation: [specific visible evidence only]
Significance: [possible investigation/security relevance, stated cautiously]
Risk: [Low / Medium / High]
Confidence: [Low / Medium / High]

Rules:
- Distinguish clearly between visible facts and hypotheses.
- Do not identify specific people by identity.
- Describe visible people as specifically as the image supports, including
  approximate age group, apparent gender presentation, clothing, uniforms,
  backpacks, bags, or other carried items when relevant.
- Prefer specific visually supported descriptions over generic terms such as
  "individual" when more informative wording is justified by the image.
- Do not infer guilt, intent, private identity, emotions, or roles such as
  student, employee, suspect, resident, victim, or offender unless the visual
  evidence supports that inference.
- Do not infer criminal intent from clothing, a hood, a face covering, or
  concealment-related appearance alone. These may be security-relevant
  observations but require additional context.
- Describe interactions, posture, movement, carried objects, and spatial
  relationships when visually supported.
- If an object is unclear, describe it as ambiguous rather than naming it as
  a weapon, tool, stolen item, or other specific object without sufficient
  visual evidence.
- If an object could plausibly be weapon-like but cannot be identified
  confidently, explicitly state the uncertainty.
- Do not invent crimes, threats, violence, theft, concealment, fraud, or other
  events that are not visually supported.
- Do not use timestamps, watermarks, news logos, surveillance-camera appearance,
  or media-source overlays as evidence that a crime occurred.
- These metadata elements may be reported only when relevant for source,
  provenance, or timeline analysis.
- Do not treat the absence of visible evidence as proof that an event did or
  did not occur.
- If the image contains potentially concerning cues but the event itself
  cannot be established, state that explicitly.
- If the scene appears benign, state that no clear investigation-relevant risk
  is visible instead of forcing a suspicious interpretation.
- Prefer 1–5 non-repetitive findings.
- Mention uncertainty explicitly when evidence is weak.
- Avoid irrelevant decoration or aesthetic description unless it helps establish
  location, environment, chronology, or another investigative context.
